How do RNNs encode mixtures?

One network yields distinct geometries.

Esther Poniatowski · Claire Sergent

Integrative Neuroscience and Cognition Center (INCC), CNRS UMR 8002, Université Paris Cité

Natural inputs often mix simpler components, and single neurons respond to such mixtures heterogeneously. Across a whole network, however, the code becomes interpretable. Two questions follow:

Which distinct codes for mixtures are possible, a priori?
Mixture geometry varies along two independent properties: its balance relative to the input, and the emergence of a pattern outside the components' plane.
Which network mechanisms select the actual code?
Two successive transformations of the input control which geometry arises: a feedforward projection onto the network's collective coordinates, then a recurrent amplification of them.

Details address the following points:

  1. 1Modelthe recurrent network, its structured connectivity, and the collective coordinates it reduces to
  2. 2Two-transformation response mapthe input is projected into collective coordinates, then amplified by recurrent feedback
  3. 3Coordinate factorizationalong each principal direction, the response is a drive rescaled by an amplification factor
  4. 4Condition for pure scalinguniform amplification keeps the mixture aligned with the proportional sum
  5. 5Condition for in-plane geometrywhat keeps a rotated response inside the plane the components span
  6. 6Input variance vs. similaritywhy superposing two inputs shifts the gain, and how dissimilarity sets the shift
  7. 7Divisive normalization vs. network modelwhere the two agree, and where the circuit bias saturates while the power law grows
